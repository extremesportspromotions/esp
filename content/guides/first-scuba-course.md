Learning to scuba dive opens up a whole new world — quite literally. Whether you dream of tropical reefs or want to explore the wrecks and marine life around the British coast, it all starts with an entry-level course. Here's what happens on one, and how to choose a course that suits you.

## Try it before you sign up

If you've never breathed underwater before, a try dive is a great first step. These are short, supervised sessions, usually in a swimming pool, where an instructor shows you the basics and lets you experience breathing from scuba equipment in shallow water. PADI calls its version Discover Scuba Diving, and BSAC clubs commonly run try dives too.

It's a low-commitment way to find out whether you enjoy it before paying for a full course.

## The main entry-level courses

There are several training organisations, known as agencies, and their entry-level qualifications are recognised internationally. The ones you're most likely to come across in the UK are:

- **PADI Open Water Diver.** Usually taught through dive centres. The minimum age is 10, with 10 to 14-year-olds earning a Junior Open Water Diver certification. The course combines theory (often done online as eLearning), five confined-water dives in a pool or similar conditions, and four open water dives with your instructor.
- **BSAC Ocean Diver.** The British Sub-Aqua Club's entry-level course is open to anyone from the age of 12 and is traditionally taught through BSAC clubs. It includes six theory modules, at least five pool or sheltered-water sessions, and at least four open water dives. It also introduces rescue skills and the use of nitrox from the start.
- **SSI Open Water Diver.** Scuba Schools International runs a similar entry-level course through its dive centres.

All three teach the same core skills, including using your equipment, controlling your buoyancy, handling simple problems underwater and planning safe dives. Each agency sets its own depth limit for newly qualified divers, and your instructor will explain what yours means in practice.

## What happens on the course

**Theory.** You'll learn how pressure affects your body and your equipment, how to plan a dive, and how to stay safe. Depending on the course, this might be eLearning at home, classroom sessions or a mix of both. Expect short quizzes and a final assessment.

**Pool (confined water) training.** This is where you'll first breathe underwater, then practise essential skills in a controlled environment — such as clearing water from your mask, recovering your regulator (mouthpiece) and controlling your buoyancy — until they feel natural.

**Open water dives.** Finally, you'll repeat those skills in open water, such as the sea, a lake or an inland dive site, with your instructor watching closely. This is where it all comes together.

You'll also need to show you're comfortable in the water. PADI's water skills assessment includes a swim and a 10-minute float, while BSAC asks you to show you can comfortably swim 200 metres in a pool.

## Medical checks

Before any in-water training, you'll complete a medical questionnaire. If you answer "yes" to any of the questions, you'll need a suitably qualified doctor to confirm you're fit to dive before you get in the water. In UK clubs, this is handled through the UK Diving Medical Committee's self-declaration process. Answer honestly — the questions are there to protect you.

> [!SAFETY]
> The most important rule in scuba diving is to breathe continuously and never hold your breath. Don't dive if you're feeling unwell, especially with a cold or congestion, as this can make equalising your ears painful or impossible. Always dive within the limits of your training, and ask your instructor how long you should wait before flying after diving.

## Dive centre or club?

In the UK, you'll usually choose between a dive centre and a club.

**Dive centres** are businesses that run courses to a set schedule. They can often get you qualified relatively quickly, and many sell or hire equipment.

**Clubs**, such as BSAC branches, are mostly run by volunteer instructors. Training tends to be spread over weekly pool sessions, and the big advantage is that you join a ready-made group of people to dive with after you qualify.

Neither is better; it depends on your schedule, budget and whether you want a community to dive with afterwards.

## Learning in the UK or abroad?

Learning in UK waters means cooler temperatures and often lower visibility, so you'll wear a thick wetsuit or a drysuit. That can feel more demanding at first, but divers who learn here are well prepared for British conditions. Others choose to learn somewhere warmer on holiday. Some agencies let you complete the theory and pool training at home and your open water dives elsewhere — ask about a "referral" if that appeals.

## How to choose a course

Before you book, ask:

- How big are the groups, and how many students does each instructor supervise in the water?
- Where do the pool sessions and open water dives take place?
- What equipment is included, and what will I need to buy or hire? Many students buy their own mask, snorkel, fins and boots, as a good fit matters.
- Are there extra costs, such as site entry fees, eLearning or certification?
- What's the schedule, and what happens if I miss a session or the weather cancels a dive?
- What can I do after I qualify?

A good instructor will be happy to answer all of these and won't rush you.
