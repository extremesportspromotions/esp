A tandem skydive is the simplest way to experience freefall for the first time. You don't need any previous experience or a long training course: you're securely attached to a qualified instructor who takes care of the technical side [1]. Here's what to expect, from booking to landing.

## How a tandem skydive works

On a tandem you're harnessed to a professional instructor, who controls the exit, freefall, parachute deployment and landing [1]. You climb to altitude in an aircraft and exit together. British Skydiving gives typical figures of around 10,000–15,000 feet for the exit, around 30–60 seconds of freefall and a 5–7 minute parachute flight back to the landing area [1]. Exact heights and timings vary between centres, so ask yours when you book.

Tandem instructors hold their own British Skydiving rating. Trainees first qualify as Tandem Basic Instructors, who can brief you and fit your harness but can't take students on real tandem jumps, before becoming full Tandem Instructors [2]. Tandem parachutes are fitted with automatic activation devices, and equipment is professionally maintained and inspected [1]. One centre, Skydive Langar, explains that its parachute systems carry two parachutes, a main and a reserve [3].

## Before you book: age, health and weight

In the UK, tandem jumps take place at drop zones affiliated to **British Skydiving** and are run to its standards and procedures [1][4]. Its key requirements are:

- **Age.** You must be at least 16. If you're under 18, you need your parent's or guardian's written permission [1], and your medical declaration must be witnessed by your parent or legal guardian [5].
- **Upper age.** There's no upper age limit for tandem skydiving; your overall health is the key factor [1]. Individual centres may have their own checks: GoSkydive, for example, asks jumpers aged 70 or over to get in touch for a pre-jump assessment [6].
- **Health.** Every tandem participant completes a medical self-declaration [1], British Skydiving's Form 115A. If you can't agree with every statement on it, you should postpone your jump until you've had qualified medical advice and a doctor has signed Form 115B [5]. Some centres won't accept a doctor's letter instead of the form [6]. The centre and your instructor can't give medical advice [5].
- **Weight.** Each centre sets its own weight limits based on its risk assessments. British Skydiving says maximums are typically around 15 stone, and it doesn't keep a list, so contact the centre directly [1]. Limits really do vary: as of September 2026, Skydive Langar's limit is 15 stone (95 kg), while GoSkydive accepts jumpers up to 18 stone after checking suitability, with a surcharge above 15 stone [3][6].

If you're unsure about anything health-related, speak to your GP well before the day.

## Choosing a centre

Choose a centre affiliated to British Skydiving, known as an Affiliated Parachute Training Organisation (PTO). British Skydiving says there are 27 in the UK, all following the same safety standards, and its website has a "Find your nearest PTO" tool [4]. Beyond that, ask:

- What's included in the price, and are photos or video extra?
- How long should I expect to be on site?
- What happens if the weather stops jumping on my booked day?
- Are there any restrictions on my height, weight or fitness?

If you're jumping for charity, ask both the charity and the centre how sponsored jumps work.

## On the day

Centres suggest allowing several hours: Skydive Langar recommends 3 hours from arrival, and GoSkydive says to plan for 3–5 hours and keep the rest of your day flexible [3][6]. You'll check in, hand over your medical form and complete the centre's paperwork. Form 115A must be handed to the centre in person, not sent to British Skydiving, and you'll re-confirm it just before your jump [5].

Next comes your briefing. British Skydiving says it covers safety procedures, equipment and what to expect [4]. At Skydive Langar, it takes about 10–15 minutes [3]. Listen carefully, especially to what your instructor says about your body position and landing.

Then you may have to wait. Skydiving is very weather-dependent: centres must operate within strict British Skydiving limits on cloud and wind, and if conditions aren't suitable you'll be offered another date [3]. Bring food, water and something to pass the time.

> [!SAFETY]
> Be completely honest on your medical form. You must tell the instructors if you've ever partly or fully dislocated a shoulder; it doesn't automatically stop you jumping, but it lets your instructor help reduce the risk of it happening again [5]. Colds or sore throats can make you temporarily unfit to jump [5], and anyone believed to be under the influence of alcohol or drugs won't be allowed to jump [6]. Follow every instruction in your briefing, and ask about anything that's unclear before you board.

## The jump itself

After the climb (about 15 minutes at Skydive Langar [3]), you'll leave the aircraft with your instructor in the position you were briefed on. Freefall is over quickly [1]. Skydive Langar says many people expect a rollercoaster "sinking" feeling, but that most describe it as feeling more like flying [3].

Your instructor then deploys the parachute for a canopy ride of several minutes, with time to take in the view [1]. For landing, do exactly what you practised in your briefing.

## What to wear and bring

- Comfortable clothes you can move in, suited to the weather, with warm layers and gloves on a cold day [3][6]. GoSkydive provides a jumpsuit and asks you to avoid high collars and zips [6].
- Flat, tight-fitting shoes with no hooks, heels or buckles; laced trainers are best [6].
- If you wear glasses, goggles that fit over them are available at centres such as GoSkydive and Skydive Langar; tell your instructor if you wear contact lenses [3][6].
- Your completed medical form and booking details.
- Food, water and something to do during weather holds.

## If you catch the bug

Many people jump once for the experience; others want more [4]. British Skydiving offers two routes to learning to skydive solo: Accelerated Freefall (AFF) and the Category System (often called static line). Many people qualify for a British Skydiving A Licence in around 18 jumps [4].
