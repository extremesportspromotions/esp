A tandem skydive is the quickest way to experience freefall. You don't need any previous experience: you're securely attached to a qualified tandem instructor who handles the parachute, the timing and the landing, while you get to enjoy the ride. Here's what to expect, from booking through to your feet touching the grass again.

## How a tandem skydive works

On a tandem jump you wear a harness that clips to the front of your instructor's harness. The instructor wears the parachute system — a main canopy plus a reserve — and is trained and rated specifically for carrying passengers.

You'll climb to altitude in a small aircraft, leave the plane together, fall for a period of freefall, and then the instructor opens the parachute for a gentler descent under the canopy. The exact exit height, and therefore the length of your freefall, depends on the centre and the aircraft it flies, so ask when you book.

## Before you book: age, health and weight

In the UK, tandem skydiving is overseen by **British Skydiving**, the sport's governing body. A few of its requirements are worth knowing early:

- **Age.** The minimum age is 16. If you're 16 or 17, a parent or legal guardian has to give consent and witness your medical declaration.
- **Health.** Everyone completes a British Skydiving medical self-declaration form. If you have any of the conditions or injuries listed on it, you'll need a separate form signed by your doctor instead. Only the official forms are accepted, so don't rely on a letter from your GP.
- **Height and weight.** Each centre sets its own limits, as these depend on its equipment. Check with the centre before you book rather than assuming.

There's generally no upper age limit for a tandem, but older jumpers may be asked for medical advice first. If you're unsure about anything health-related, speak to your GP well before the day — your instructor isn't able to give medical advice.

## Choosing a centre

Look for a centre affiliated to British Skydiving (known in the sport as a Parachute Training Organisation). Beyond that, it's worth asking a few practical questions:

- What's included in the price, and are photos or video an optional extra?
- How long should I expect to be on site?
- What happens if the weather stops jumping on my booked day?
- Are there any restrictions on my height, weight or fitness?

If you're jumping for charity, many centres and charities run sponsored jump schemes, so check whether your chosen charity works with a particular centre.

## On the day

Plan to give the whole day to it. You'll check in, hand over your medical form, and complete the centre's own paperwork. Then comes your briefing, where your instructor explains the body position you'll use for the exit and freefall, what happens when the parachute opens, and — most importantly — how to hold your legs for landing. Listen carefully; it's short, but it matters.

You'll then be fitted with a harness, and often a jumpsuit, goggles and a hat or helmet.

Then, quite possibly, you'll wait. Skydiving depends on the weather: low cloud, strong or gusty wind, and rain can all pause jumping. Waiting is completely normal at UK drop zones and is a sign the centre is taking conditions seriously. Bring snacks, water, something to read and a bit of patience.

> [!SAFETY]
> Be completely honest on your medical form, and tell your instructor about any previous injuries — especially a dislocated shoulder — even if you think they're minor. Avoid alcohol the night before, get a good night's sleep, and follow every instruction in your briefing. If anything is unclear, ask before you board, not at the door.

## The jump itself

The climb is often the part people find most nerve-racking. Your instructor will tighten and check the connections between your harnesses and run through the briefing with you again.

When the door opens, it gets loud, cold and windy. You'll shuffle to the door together, adopt the position you practised, and then you're out. Freefall is intense: there's a rush of air and noise, and with no nearby ground to judge speed against, it tends to feel more like a powerful wind than falling. Try to breathe, keep your position, and look around — it goes quickly.

When your instructor opens the parachute, you'll feel a firm deceleration, and then everything goes quiet. The canopy ride is calm, with time to take in the view. Some instructors will let you help steer.

For landing, you'll do what you practised in the briefing — usually lifting your legs so your instructor can land first. Most tandem landings are gentle, finishing in a slide or a few steps.

## What to wear and bring

- Comfortable, practical clothes suited to the weather, with a warm layer — it's colder at altitude.
- Secure trainers. Avoid boots with hooks, as they can snag on equipment.
- If you wear glasses or contact lenses, tell the centre; goggles designed to go over glasses are usually available.
- Your completed medical form, your booking details and anything else the centre asks for.
- Food, water and something to pass the time if there are weather holds.

Leave loose jewellery and anything in your pockets on the ground.

## If you catch the bug

Plenty of people do one tandem and are happy to tick it off the list. If you want more, UK centres offer solo training courses, such as Accelerated Freefall (AFF), which teach you to skydive on your own and work towards a qualification. A tandem is a great way to find out whether that path is for you.
