Paragliding is about as close as most of us will get to flying like a bird: no engine, just a fabric wing, a harness and the air moving over the hills. The UK has a long-established free-flying community, and learning here gives you a solid grounding for flying almost anywhere. The key is to learn properly from the start.

## Start with a BHPA-registered school

In the UK, hang gliding and paragliding are overseen by the **British Hang Gliding and Paragliding Association (BHPA)**. Initial training has to be done at a BHPA-registered school, and that's where your journey should begin.

Registered schools teach to a common standard, so the level you're expected to reach is the same wherever you train. What varies is the method: the type of site, the launch technique and, very much in the UK, the weather on the day.

The BHPA website has a "find a school" search, and our own map shows paragliding schools and clubs we've checked.

## Try it first

If you're not sure paragliding is for you, you don't have to commit to a full course straight away. Many schools offer a short taster or introductory day, and qualified tandem pilots offer tandem flights where you fly as a passenger. Either will give you a feel for the sport, and a taster day is a good chance to see how a school operates before you book further training.

## How training works

Training is progressive, and every stage builds on the one before.

**Ground handling.** You'll start by learning to control the wing on the ground: laying it out, inflating it above your head and keeping it there. It's harder than it looks and takes practice, but it's the foundation of everything else.

**First hops.** Once you can control the wing on the ground, you'll learn to take off and land. Your first flights are short, straight lines only a few feet above a gentle slope, with an instructor guiding you by radio.

**Higher flights and turns.** As you show you can get into the air and down again safely, your instructor will move you to higher take-off points, which gives you the height to start learning to turn.

**Theory.** Alongside the flying, you'll cover the basics of how a wing flies, weather, air law and flying rules, and sit simple exams.

## The two training milestones

The BHPA's Pilot Rating Scheme gives you a clear ladder to climb:

- **Elementary Pilot (EP).** This progress award marks the end of the introductory stage and shows you're ready for further training.
- **Club Pilot (Novice) (CP).** This is your first rating proper. It marks the end of formal instruction at a school and means you're qualified to leave the school and fly within a BHPA club.

Some schools teach on a hill; others teach in an airfield environment, using a winch to tow you into the air. Both routes lead to the same ratings, so ask schools which method they use and which suits where you live.

> [!SAFETY]
> Never try to teach yourself, or fly a borrowed wing without proper instruction. Paragliding carries real risk, and most of it comes down to judgement: knowing when conditions are safe, and when to stay on the ground. Fly only within your rating, respect your instructor's decisions about the weather, and remember that "not today" is always an acceptable answer.

## Weather, time and patience

The UK's weather is the biggest variable in learning to paraglide. Wind that's too strong, from the wrong direction, or gusty — or simply rain and low cloud — will ground training. That means courses are often spread over a number of days or weekends, and you may need to be flexible about dates.

It's worth asking schools how they handle unflyable days, how long it typically takes students to complete each stage, and whether your course price covers training until you reach a milestone or a fixed number of days.

## Age and fitness

By law, you must be at least 14 before you can fly solo, and many schools set their own higher minimum age, often around 16. You don't need to be an athlete, but you should be reasonably fit: you'll be walking up hills carrying equipment, and you'll need to run during take-off. If you have any health concerns, speak to the school and your GP before you start.

## Kit: don't rush to buy

Schools provide the equipment you need during training, so there's no need to buy a wing on day one. When the time comes, get advice from your instructor or club coaches. A wing has to be suitable for both your weight and your ability, and a wing that's too advanced for you is a genuine safety risk. For training, you'll typically need sturdy boots with good ankle support and clothing suitable for being outdoors on an exposed hill.

## Choosing a school

When comparing schools, ask about:

- where they train, and whether they teach on a hill or with a winch tow;
- how many students each instructor looks after;
- what the course price includes, and what happens if the weather is poor;
- whether they have links with local clubs for when you qualify.

## After Club Pilot

Gaining your Club Pilot rating isn't the end of learning — it's the start of flying independently with support. You'll join a BHPA recreational club, where club coaches can guide your progression and help you work towards your next rating, Pilot. Clubs look after local flying sites and their rules, so you'll also learn where and when each site works.
